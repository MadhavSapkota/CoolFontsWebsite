Kaomoji: Meaning, Examples and How to Copy and Type Them

Updated November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Kaomoji are Japanese-style text faces built from letters, symbols and punctuation, such as (◕‿◕) or ¯\_(ツ)_/¯. Unlike Western emoticons, you read them upright, not sideways. This guide explains what they are, gives copy-ready faces by mood, and shows how to type them on Android.

What are kaomoji?

Meaning of the word kaomoji

The word comes from Japanese: kao means face and moji means character, so kaomoji literally means “face characters”. People also call them Japanese emoticons or text faces. They are not images. They are a row of ordinary text characters arranged so that they look like a face with an expression.

Why they face forward instead of sideways

The Western emoticon :) asks you to tilt your head to the left. Japanese-style faces put the eyes in the middle of the line and the mouth between or below them, so the face looks straight at you. That layout leaves room for extra details such as cheeks, arms, tears, blush marks and sparkles. This is why these faces can be much more expressive than a two- or three-character emoticon.

How text faces are built from Unicode characters

The building blocks: eyes, mouths, arms and effects

Most of these faces are assembled from Unicode characters, the same standard that covers every letter and symbol on your phone. A typical face has these parts:

  • Outline: parentheses or brackets, such as ( ) or ｢ ｣.
  • Eyes: symbols like ◕, ˘, ^, ・ or ಥ (a letter from the Kannada script, borrowed for its teary look).
  • Mouth: characters like ‿, ω, ▽ or _.
  • Arms and effects: ノ, ヽ, ♡, ✧, ~ and ﾟ for waving, cheering, sparkles or blushing.

Many parts come from Japanese kana, math, box-drawing and punctuation blocks, while others, like ಥ, come from scripts used for entirely different languages. None of them was designed as a face part, so the whole style is a creative reuse of existing characters.

Why one face can look different on different devices

Because these faces pull characters from several parts of Unicode, whether they display well depends on the fonts installed on the device and used by the app. If one character is missing from the font, you may see an empty box, a question mark, or a substitute symbol from another font that is a slightly different size. The face then looks off or broken. Spacing can also change from app to app.

How to type kaomoji on Android

The copy and paste method and its drawbacks

The basic way works on any Android phone:

  1. Find a kaomoji on a web page or in a list like the one above.
  2. Press and hold to select it, then choose Copy.
  3. Switch to your messaging app, caption or bio, press and hold in the text box, and choose Paste.

It works, but it is slow if you use kaomoji often. You switch apps every time, and you need to remember where you found the face you liked.

Why kaomoji sometimes show as boxes or break

Missing glyphs and font support

Since these faces mix characters from different Unicode blocks, one rare symbol can be enough to cause a problem. A device or app whose font lacks that character may show a box (often called “tofu”), a blank, or a mismatched symbol. Older devices and unusual fonts are more likely to have gaps. Faces made from very common characters, such as ^_^ or (T_T), are the safest.

Apps that change or strip special characters

Some apps, forms and username fields limit which characters they accept. They may remove symbols, replace them, or reject the entry altogether. Apps that support Markdown-style formatting can also read the backslash and underscores in the shrug as formatting marks. Depending on the app, the face may then lose its arm, drop a character, or show part of it in italics. If that happens, it is the app interpreting formatting characters, not an error on your side.

Tips: test before posting and have a simpler fallback

  • Paste the face into a draft or a chat with yourself and check how it looks.
  • If part of it breaks, pick a simpler face, or replace the odd symbols with common ones.
  • Remember that the person reading your message may see it differently on their own device.

Using kaomoji accessibly and with good taste

How screen readers may read them

A screen reader does not see a smiley, it sees a string of characters. Depending on the software, it may read each symbol aloud by name, skip some, or produce a long jumble. A single face can therefore take much longer to hear than an ordinary emoji. We have not tested specific screen readers for this guide, so treat this as a general caution and not a measured result.

Keep the message in words, use the face as an accent

Write the meaning of your message in words, and add the face at the end like a bit of punctuation: “Thanks so much for the help (＾▽＾)”. Avoid replacing words with faces, stacking several in a row, or putting them in the middle of sentences, since all of that makes text harder to follow by ear and by eye.

Match the face to the platform and audience

Playful faces suit friends, fan communities, casual captions and bios. For work emails, official notices or serious conversations, plain text is usually the better choice. Can you make your own kaomoji?

Mixing and matching parts

Yes. Because a text face is only a row of characters, you can swap parts. Start with a frame like ( ), then choose eyes, a mouth and an accessory:

  • Take (◕‿◕) and change the eyes to ˘ for (˘‿˘), which looks sleepy and content.
  • Add arms and sparkles: ヽ(˘‿˘)ﾉ✧.
  • Swap the mouth for ω to get a cat-like look: (˘ω˘).

Keep it readable

Simple designs work best. The more unusual the characters, the more likely they are to show as boxes on someone else's phone. Check your creation on at least one other app before you rely on it, and keep a plainer version in reserve.

Frequently asked questions

Are kaomoji the same as emoji?

No. Emoji are usually a single character or a short sequence that each device draws as a small picture, while kaomoji are strings of several ordinary text characters arranged into a face. Emoji tend to keep the same meaning everywhere, whereas text faces can look different depending on fonts.

Do kaomoji work on every app?

Not always. Most apps will show common faces correctly, but some fields strip or replace special characters, and a device may lack the font for a rare symbol. Test a face in a draft first, and use a simpler one if it breaks.

Are kaomoji only Japanese?

The style and the name are Japanese, and many faces use Japanese characters such as ツ or ノ. But anyone can use them, and people in many languages share and invent them. Think of them as a style of text art that started in Japan.
