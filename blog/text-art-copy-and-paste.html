Text Art Copy and Paste: Faces, Dividers, Symbols and Small ASCII Art

Updated November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Text art is a picture or decoration made from ordinary characters and symbols, and you can copy and paste it anywhere that accepts text. Below you will find copyable faces, dividers and symbol shapes, plus the reasons text art often breaks after pasting and how to avoid that.

What is text art?

Text art means building images, faces or decorations out of keyboard characters and Unicode symbols. The result is still plain text, which is why it can be pasted into a chat, note or caption without uploading an image. It is also why it can behave unpredictably: the app you paste into decides the font, spacing and line handling.

ASCII art vs Unicode symbol art

Classic ASCII art uses only the basic characters found on a standard English keyboard: letters, digits and punctuation such as / \ | _ - ( ). Unicode art uses a much larger pool of characters, such as stars, box lines, hearts and decorative marks. Unicode art can look more polished, but it depends on the device having a glyph for each character. ASCII art is plainer, but it is less likely to show up as empty boxes.

Text art vs fancy text fonts

Fancy text is a different thing. Styles like bold script or gothic lettering swap your letters for look-alike Unicode characters. They are not installed fonts, and they change how your words look. Text art builds shapes and decoration around or instead of words. If you want styled lettering rather than pictures, see our fancy text generator guide.

Where kaomoji fit in

Kaomoji are Japanese-style text faces that are read upright, face-on, like (◕‿◕). Western emoticons such as :-) are the ones you read by tilting your head sideways. Kaomoji are a compact kind of text art that fits on one line, so they avoid the alignment and line-break problems of multi-line pictures. Many of them use less common Unicode characters, though, so they can still show as boxes on some devices. Our kaomoji guide covers their meanings in more depth.

How to copy and paste text art without breaking it

Select every line, including the spaces

On Android, long-press the first character, drag the selection handles to cover the whole piece, then tap Copy. Make sure you include blank space at the start and end of lines, because ASCII art uses spaces as part of the picture. Missing a leading space is a common reason a pasted drawing looks lopsided.

Check whether the app keeps line breaks

Some places accept new lines and others flatten everything onto one line. A chat message box usually keeps line breaks, while a single-line field such as a name field does not. If the field only holds one line, use a one-line piece such as a kaomoji or divider instead.

Use a monospace or code-style format where the app supports one

ASCII art is designed for monospace fonts, where every character is the same width. Some apps and note tools offer a code or monospace format, and when that is available it keeps columns lined up far better than normal text does. If an app has no such option, keep your art small and simple.

Preview before you post

Paste into a draft, a note to yourself or a private chat first. Check it at the actual screen width, because a piece that fits one line on your phone may wrap on a smaller one.

Why text art looks broken or shows up as boxes

Proportional fonts shift the alignment

Most apps display text in a proportional font, where an i is narrower than an m. ASCII art drawn in a monospace font therefore drifts out of shape, because the columns no longer line up.

Spaces and line breaks get trimmed or wrapped

Some fields collapse repeated spaces, remove blank lines or trim leading spaces. A line that is too wide also wraps onto the next line and tears the picture apart. Narrower art survives better. Single-line pieces avoid most of these layout problems, but not the glyph problem below.

Missing glyphs on some devices and apps

Unicode characters only display if the device's fonts include them. When they do not, you see an empty box or a question mark in place of the symbol. Plain ASCII characters are the safest. Common symbols like ★ and ♡ are widely supported, while rarer decorative marks, including some used in kaomoji, are more likely to fail. You cannot tell in advance how a piece will look on someone else's phone.

Character and line limits

Bios, names and captions often have length caps, and some also cap the number of lines. Art that looks fine in a note may be cut off. If you are close to a limit, shrink the art rather than risk losing the end of it.

Where text art works and where to avoid it

Chats and messages

This is the friendliest place for it. Kaomoji and short dividers work well, and line breaks are usually kept. Large ASCII pictures can still wrap on a narrow screen, so test them first.

Bios, captions and profiles

Short lines of symbols and a divider tend to work better than big drawings. Multi-line art may be flattened or limited depending on the profile field, so check the preview before saving. For styled lettering in profile text, our fonts for Instagram guide goes into more detail.

Usernames and names

Names are short and often restrict which characters are allowed, so text art can be rejected or look odd. A single symbol is usually safer than a face. Some places also use names for search or mentions, which symbols can make harder.

Notes, documents and anything professional

Avoid decorative art in work emails, applications, formal documents and anything that must be read clearly. Plain text is easier to read, search and copy.

Keep text art readable and accessible

Why screen readers can read symbols one by one

Screen readers work on the underlying characters. Depending on the software, a row of symbols may be announced character by character, announced by name, or skipped. A long ASCII drawing can turn into a stream of meaningless punctuation.

Keep art short and put the message in plain text

Write what you want to say in normal words and treat the art as decoration. One face at the end of a sentence is far easier to listen to than a block of symbols with a sentence buried inside it. Put decoration at the end of a line, not in the middle of a sentence.

Avoid using text art for essential information

Do not use it for names, dates, prices, links or instructions. If a reader misses the decoration they lose nothing, but if they miss essential information they lose the point. The same caution applies to styled Unicode lettering.

How to make your own simple text art

Start with a small grid and basic characters

Plan a picture only a few lines tall and a dozen or so characters wide. Use characters such as / \ | _ - ( ) o . ^ and build up a shape line by line. Each line should be aligned by counting spaces from the left.

Test it in the app where you will post it

Because fonts differ between apps, always test where the art will be used. If a line drifts, adjust the spaces or simplify the shape, and keep a copy of the working version in a note.

Keep it simple so it survives on different screens

Fewer lines and fewer unusual characters mean fewer things can go wrong. A short symbol pattern or one-line face is less likely to fall apart than a detailed picture. If your idea is mostly hearts, the love symbols page has ready-made sets.

Frequently asked questions

Is text art the same as ASCII art?

Not exactly. ASCII art is one kind of text art that uses only the basic characters on a standard keyboard. Text art is the broader term and also covers Unicode symbol designs, dividers and kaomoji faces.

Why does my text art look different on my friend's phone?

Each app and device chooses its own font and decides how to handle spaces, line breaks and symbols. A character missing from the receiving device's fonts may show as a box, and a proportional font can shift multi-line art out of alignment. Single-line art avoids the alignment problem, and plain or very common characters are the least likely to turn into boxes.

Can I put multi-line text art in a bio?

It depends on the field. Some bios keep line breaks and others flatten them or limit the length, so a large drawing may be cut off or distorted. Preview it before saving, and fall back to a short one-line design if it breaks.

Is text art accessible?

Often not very. Screen readers may read symbols out one by one, name them, or skip them, so long art can be confusing. Keep it short, use it as decoration only, and put the real message in plain words.
