Copy and Paste Face Symbols: Smileys, Emoticons and Text Faces

Updated November 22, 2026 · 7 min read

Face symbols are text characters or short character combinations that look like a smiling, sad or surprised face. Copy any item below, then paste it into a chat, bio or note. This page sorts them by type, because a single-character smiley, a classic emoticon and a kaomoji each behave differently once pasted.

Face symbols to copy and paste

Tap a face to copy it. If tapping does not copy on your device, press and hold to select it instead. How a face looks depends on the font of the app you paste into, so check the result before you post.

Single-character smileys and frowns (☺ ☻ ☹)

These are single Unicode characters, so they take up one slot like a letter. Unicode includes a white smiling face, a black smiling face and a white frowning face. On some devices, a few of these can be drawn in a colorful emoji style instead of plain text, so the same symbol may not look identical everywhere.

Classic emoticons (:) :( ;) :D)

Western emoticons are built from ordinary keyboard characters, read sideways. Because they use only basic letters and punctuation, they display almost anywhere. Some apps may convert them into emoji automatically, which is a feature of that app, not of the text.

Compact text faces (^_^ -_- o_O)

These read upright rather than sideways, and they are short enough to drop into a sentence. They are made from plain characters, so they are usually safe in most fonts.

Characters that look like smiles (ツ ッ ㋡ 〠)

These were not designed as smileys. ツ and ッ are Japanese katakana letters (a large and a small "tsu"), and ㋡ is a circled version of the same letter. Their shapes happen to resemble a face with closed eyes and a smile, which is why people use them as signatures. 〠 is a postal mark that happens to have "face" in its Unicode name. Since they are letters or marks from other scripts, they depend on a font that includes them.

Real Unicode face characters vs look-alike letters

☺, ☻ and ☹ are characters that Unicode names as faces. ツ is a letter that merely resembles one. In practice both are text, so you can copy them, but a look-alike letter is more likely to need a font that supports its script.

Why emoji can look different on every device

An emoji is one agreed character, but each platform draws its own artwork for it. A grinning face on one phone can look noticeably different on another. Text faces made from punctuation avoid that difference, though they are subject to fonts instead.

When to choose a kaomoji instead

If you want a face with arms, blushing cheeks or a clear mood such as shy, angry or excited, a kaomoji gives you more to work with. Our kaomoji guide has long lists sorted by mood, so they are not repeated here.

Keeping the result short enough to stay readable

Two or three parts on each side of the eyes are plenty. Long faces break across lines, look cluttered in small text, and increase the chance that one rare character renders as a box.

Which face symbol should you use where?

Chats and comments

Anything works here. Emoticons and compact faces are quick and friendly, and kaomoji suit playful conversations.

Bios, captions and notes

A single ☺ or ツ at the end of a line keeps things tidy. For other symbol styles, see cute fonts, symbols and kaomoji.

Usernames and display names

Check each app's rules on allowed characters first, and keep the name readable. A name made only of symbols can be hard for others to search, mention or recognize.

Formal or work messages

Stay conservative. A plain :) is usually the most you need, and often a sentence of words works better than any face.

How to copy and paste a face symbol on Android

Select, copy and paste step by step

  1. Tap a face on this page, or press and hold text on any page and drag the handles to select it.
  2. If a Copy option appears, tap it. Exact wording and layout vary by phone and app.
  3. Open the app where you want the face and tap the text box.
  4. Press and hold in the box and choose Paste.

Pasting without extra spaces or broken line breaks

If you select by hand, drag only over the characters you need, since a trailing space or line break gets copied too. Check the end of your message after pasting and delete any leftovers.

Saving the faces you use most in your own notes

Keep a short list in a notes app with your five or six favorites. Opening it is faster than searching for them each time, and you can add your own combinations from the building blocks above.

Why a face symbol shows up as a box or looks wrong

Missing glyphs in a font

Every character needs a drawing, called a glyph, in the font being used. When the font lacks one, the device may try another font, and if none has it you see an empty box or a question mark. Look-alike letters like ツ and rarer kaomoji parts are the most likely to run into this.

Text-style vs emoji-style rendering of the same character

Some symbols, such as ☺ and ☹, can appear either as a simple black-and-white glyph or as a colorful emoji. Which one you get depends on the app, the device and whether special variation characters are attached, so you cannot fully control it by copying.

Different apps and devices showing the same symbol differently

The characters you copy stay the same, but the drawing differs between fonts, so a face can be thicker, thinner or slightly different on a friend's phone. If it matters, send a test message first.

Using face symbols accessibly

How screen readers may read unusual symbols aloud or skip them

Screen readers work from character names or their own rules. A symbol may be spoken by its Unicode name, read oddly, or skipped altogether, depending on the software. A long kaomoji made of many unusual characters can turn into a stream of odd names.

Do not replace important words with a face

Use a face to add tone, not to carry meaning. If a message only makes sense when the face is read, someone using assistive technology, or a device missing the glyph, may miss it.

Put a face at the end of a sentence, not in the middle

At the end, a skipped or garbled face costs the reader very little. Use one or two at most per message.

Frequently asked questions

Are face symbols the same as emoji?

No. A face symbol is a text character or a short group of characters that your font draws like any letter. An emoji is a character that your device draws as a small picture, and its artwork differs between platforms. Some symbols such as ☺ can appear in either style depending on the app.

Is ツ an official smiley?

No. ツ is the Japanese katakana letter "tsu". Its shape looks like a smiling face, so people borrow it as a smiley, but Unicode does not treat it as a face character.

Why do some faces look different on a friend's phone?

The characters are identical, but each device and app uses its own fonts and emoji artwork to draw them. If a font does not include a character, the phone may substitute another font or show a box.

Can I make my own face symbol?

Yes. Combine a frame such as parentheses, a matching pair of eyes and a mouth, for example (˘ω˘) or (◕‿◕). Keep it short and test it in the app where you plan to use it, because rarer characters may not display everywhere.
